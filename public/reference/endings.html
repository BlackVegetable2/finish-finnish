Finnish adds endings to words where English uses little words like in, to, of or my. This page spells the endings out so you can scroll through or jump to what you need. I wrote it from general knowledge of Finnish grammar, so check the rarer details against a textbook.

1. Vowel harmony: which spelling of the ending

Many endings come in two spellings, one with a / o / u and one with ä / ö / y: -ssa / -ssä, -lla / -llä, and so on.

  • If the word has a, o or u, use the -a version: talo → talossa (in the house), kissa → kissalla.
  • If it has ä, ö or y (and no a/o/u), use the -ä version: pöytä → pöydässä (at the table), työ → työssä (at work).
  • If it has only e or i (neutral vowels), use the -ä version: meri → meressä (in the sea), kieli → kielessä (in the language).

Stem changes: the ending often attaches to a slightly changed word stem. The most common change is consonant gradation: a strong consonant softens (kk → k, pp → p, tt → t, k → (nothing), p → v, t → d, nk → ng), for example kauppa → kaupassa (in the shop), pöytä → pöydällä, kaupunki → kaupungissa. Many words also change their last vowel, which is why you learn each noun's stem as you meet it (Suomi → Suomessa, nainen → naisessa).

3. Nominative and the plural

Nominative has no ending. It's the dictionary form, and what you use for the subject of a sentence: Talo on iso. = The house is big.

4. Genitive: -n

Possession, like English "of" or "'s". It's also the form that goes before many "position words".

  • Mikon auto = Mikko's car
  • talon ovi = the door of the house
  • With position words: pöydän alla = under the table, talon takana = behind the house, Mikon kanssa = with Mikko

Plural: -en / -jen / -ten (see above). For "my / your / his…" use the pronoun forms (minun, sinun, hänen) or the possessive endings below.

5. Accusative: the object of a verb

Finnish has no separate accusative ending of its own. A whole, finished object looks like one of three things:

  • -n (same as the genitive) for singular nouns: Näen talon. = I see the house.
  • -t for personal pronouns: Näen sinut. = I see you; minut (me), hänet (him/her), meidät, teidät, heidät.
  • No ending (looks like the nominative) for plurals, and in commands: Ostan kirjat. = I buy the books; Osta maito! = Buy the milk!

If the object is only partly or not-yet affected, it's partitive instead, which is the next section.

6. Partitive: -a / -ä, -ta / -tä

The "some of it / part of it / not finished" case. You use it:

  • For an unspecified amount (like "some"): Juon kahvia. = I drink (some) coffee, vs. Join kahvin. = I drank the (whole) coffee.
  • After numbers above one: kaksi kahvia = two coffees; kolme kirjaa = three books.
  • In negatives: Ei ole kahvia. = There is no coffee.
  • After quantity words: paljon vettä = a lot of water, vähän maitoa = a little milk.
  • With certain verbs of feeling and waiting: Rakastan sinua. = I love you; Odotan bussia. = I'm waiting for the bus.
  • Languages: Puhun suomea. = I speak Finnish.

Spelling: -a / -ä after words ending in a short vowel (talo → taloa, kahvi → kahvia); -ta / -tä after long vowels and many other stems (maa → maata, tie → tietä, työ → työtä, vesi → vettä).

7. The six place cases in detail

Inessive: -ssa / -ssä (in)

talossa = in the house; Suomessa = in Finland; kaupungissa = in the city.

Elative: -sta / -stä (out of, from, about)

talosta = out of the house; Olen kotoisin Suomesta. = I'm from Finland. Also "about": Puhumme kirjasta. = We're talking about the book; and "like": Pidän kahvista. = I like coffee.

Illative: -Vn / -hVn / -seen (into, to)

The ending repeats the last vowel and adds n: talo → taloon (into the house), Suomi → Suomeen (into Finland), kaupunki → kaupunkiin (to the city). After long vowels it is -hVn: maa → maahan, työ → työhön. For words in -nen it is -seen: nainen → naiseen. Also: kotiin = (to) home.

Adessive: -lla / -llä (on, at, with, by means of)

  • On / at: pöydällä = on the table; koululla = at the school.
  • By means of: kynällä = with a pen; bussilla = by bus.
  • "To have": Minulla on koira. = I have a dog (literally "at me is a dog"); Hänellä on kirja. = He/she has a book.
  • Time: keväällä = in the spring; tällä viikolla = this week.

Ablative: -lta / -ltä (from a surface or person)

pöydältä = from the table; Mikolta = from Mikko. Also in "sounds/looks like": Se kuulostaa hyvältä. = That sounds good; Se näyttää hyvältä. = It looks good.

Allative: -lle (onto, to, for a person)

pöydälle = onto the table; Annan sen sinulle. = I give it to you; Mikolle = to/for Mikko.

8. Essive: -na / -nä (as; on a day)

  • As (a role or state): opettajana = as a teacher; lapsena = as a child.
  • On a day of the week: maanantaina = on Monday; sunnuntaina = on Sunday.

9. Translative: -ksi (becoming; for a period; in a language)

  • Becoming: Hän opiskelee opettajaksi. = He/she is studying to become a teacher.
  • For a length of time: Olen täällä viikoksi. = I'm here for a week.
  • "In (a language)": Mitä se on suomeksi? = What is it in Finnish?

10. Abessive: -tta / -ttä (without)

rahatta = without money; sokeritta = without sugar. It's mostly written. In everyday speech people say ilman + partitive: ilman maitoa = without milk.

12. Possessive endings (my, your, his…)

Added to the end of a noun, often together with the genitive pronoun for emphasis:

EndingMeaningkirja (book)
-nimykirjani
-siyour (one person)kirjasi
-nsa / -nsähis / her / theirkirjansa
-mmeourkirjamme
-nneyour (y'all's)kirjanne
  • With a case ending, the possessive goes after it: talossani = in my house; kirjassasi = in your book.
  • For his/her/their, after a case ending it often shortens to -Vn: talossaan = in his/her own house; kotonaan = at his/her home.
  • In spoken Finnish the ending is often dropped for "my" and "your": minun kirja, or just mun kirja.

13. Little endings added to words

EndingWhat it doesExample
-ko / -kö turns a statement into a yes/no question; goes on the first word, usually the verb Onko kahvia? = Is there coffee?   Puhutko suomea? = Do you speak Finnish?
-kin also, too Minäkin tulen. = I'm coming too.
-kaan / -kään not even / (not) either; goes with a negative En minäkään tiedä. = I don't know either.   ei koskaan = never; ei kukaan = nobody
-han / -hän "you know", "after all": a soft emphasis Sehän on selvää. = Well, that's obvious.
-pa / -pä exclamations: "how …!", "indeed!" Onpa kaunis päivä! = What a beautiful day!

14. Comparing: -mpi and -in

  • -mpi = more / -er: iso → isompi (bigger), kaunis → kauniimpi (prettier). "Than" is kuin (isompi kuin minä), or the compared thing in the partitive (isompi minua).
  • -in = most / -est: isoin (biggest), kaunein (prettiest).
  • Irregular, as in English: hyvä → parempi → paras (good, better, best).

15. The rare ones

You'll mostly meet these in set phrases or formal writing.

  • Comitative: -ine- + a possessive ending — "together with", often with a whole group: lapsineen = with his/her children, vaimoineen = with his wife. In speech people use kanssa instead: lasten kanssa.
  • Instructive: -n (usually on plural nouns) — "by means of / in the manner of": omin silmin = with one's own eyes; jalan = on foot; kaikin puolin = in every way.
